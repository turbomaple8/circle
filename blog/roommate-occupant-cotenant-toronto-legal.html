Roommate vs Occupant vs Co-Tenant in Toronto: Which One Are You (and Why It Matters)

"Roommate" is a social word, not a legal one — and in Ontario the legal label on your arrangement (co-tenant, occupant, subtenant) decides your rights, your risk, and who's on the hook if things go wrong. Here's how to tell which one you actually are.

Why These Words Matter

People say 'roommate' to mean anyone they share a home with. But Ontario tenancy law doesn't recognize 'roommate' — it recognizes specific legal relationships, each with different protections and liabilities. Two people calling each other roommates can be in completely different legal positions, and that difference surfaces exactly when something goes wrong: a departure, a dispute, an eviction, a deposit fight.

Knowing your actual legal status lets you understand your rights, your exposure, and what you should get in writing. It builds directly on the owner-vs-tenant distinction (our legal split guide) and the renter-rights basics (Ontario renter rights primer).

This is a plain-language explainer, not legal advice — for a real situation, consult a tenant legal clinic or the LTB. But knowing the categories is the foundation.

Co-Tenant: On the Lease Together

A co-tenant is named on the lease alongside others. All co-tenants typically share a single tenancy — and here's the critical part: you're usually 'jointly and severally liable,' meaning the landlord can pursue ANY co-tenant for the FULL rent, not just their share. If a co-tenant stops paying or leaves, the others owe the whole amount and must chase the departed one themselves.

Co-tenants have strong RTA protections (against eviction, on deposits, rent increases) — but also shared, collective liability. It's the most common informal-share setup and the one that causes the 'roommate left, now I owe their rent' crisis covered in our early-departure guide.

Upside: full legal protection. Downside: you're financially entangled with people whose reliability you're betting on.

Occupant: Living There, Not on the Lease

An occupant lives in the unit but isn't named on the lease — think of a leaseholder's partner, friend, or informal roommate who moved in later. Occupants generally have weak or no direct standing with the landlord: limited RTA protection, no direct claim to the tenancy, and dependence on the leaseholder's relationship with the landlord.

If the leaseholder leaves or is evicted, an occupant can find themselves with no legal right to stay. If an occupant leaves, the leaseholder still owes the full rent. It's a flexible arrangement but a legally fragile one — the occupant is exposed, and the leaseholder carries the liability.

If you're moving into someone else's lease, know you're likely an occupant, not a co-tenant — and get a written roommate agreement spelling out your rights, because the RTA may not provide them. Our agreement template covers this.

Subtenant: Renting From a Tenant

A subtenant rents from an existing tenant (the sublandlord) rather than directly from the owner — common when someone leaves for a term and sublets their room. Subletting has specific Ontario rules: it generally requires the landlord's consent, the original tenant remains responsible to the landlord, and the subtenant's rights run through the sublandlord.

Subtenants are protected in some ways but sit at the end of a chain: your security depends on the sublandlord's tenancy staying intact and the sublet being properly authorized. An unauthorized sublet is a frequent source of disputes — and of scams, where someone 'sublets' a room they have no right to (our scam prevention guide).

If you're subletting, verify the sublet is landlord-authorized and get it in writing. An informal cash sublet with no paper trail is the riskiest arrangement of all.

Knowing Which You Are — and Choosing Better

Add these questions to any shared-housing decision: Am I on the lease (co-tenant), living on someone else's lease (occupant), or renting from a tenant (subtenant)? Each answer carries different rights and risks — co-tenants get protection but shared liability, occupants are flexible but exposed, subtenants depend on a chain.

For anyone who values clarity and protection — especially newcomers and students — the cleanest arrangement is an individual per-room agreement where you're neither entangled in joint liability nor dependent on a leaseholder's chain. That's structurally how professionally managed co-living and per-room room rentals work: your own agreement for your own room, no joint liability, no occupant fragility, no sublet chain.

Whichever category you're in, get it in writing, pay traceably, and know your standing before you need it. Pair this with the early-departure guide and renter rights primer for the full legal picture.
